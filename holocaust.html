The Holocaust: 1933-1945

Six million Jews systematically murdered. Two-thirds of European Jewry annihilated. The Holocaust stands as the darkest chapter in human history—a state-sponsored genocide that sought to erase an entire people from existence.

Overview

The Holocaust (Shoah in Hebrew) was the systematic, bureaucratic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million Jews by the Nazi regime and its collaborators between 1933 and 1945.

Under Adolf Hitler's leadership, the Nazis came to power in Germany in January 1933. They believed that Germans were "racially superior" and that Jews were "inferior" and a threat to the so-called German racial community.

During the Holocaust, the Nazis also targeted other groups because of their perceived racial and biological inferiority: Roma (Gypsies), people with disabilities, Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and others. They also persecuted communists, socialists, Jehovah's Witnesses, and homosexuals.

By the time Nazi Germany was defeated in 1945, six million Jews—nearly two-thirds of European Jewry—had been murdered. Entire Jewish communities across Europe were destroyed, and centuries of Jewish culture, scholarship, and tradition were obliterated.

The Holocaust was not a singular event but a process that unfolded in stages: discrimination, persecution, ghettoization, deportation, and finally, systematic murder in concentration camps and death camps specifically designed for mass killing.

Key Phases

1933-1939: Persecution & Discrimination

The Nazis gradually excluded Jews from German society through discriminatory laws. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of citizenship and prohibited marriage between Jews and non-Jews. Kristallnacht (November 1938) marked a violent escalation, with synagogues burned and Jewish businesses destroyed across Germany.

1939-1941: Ghettoization

Following the invasion of Poland, Nazis forced Jews into overcrowded ghettos where starvation and disease were rampant. The Warsaw Ghetto held over 400,000 Jews in an area of 1.3 square miles. Conditions were deliberately designed to weaken the population.

1941-1942: Mass Shootings

The invasion of the Soviet Union marked the beginning of systematic mass murder. Einsatzgruppen (mobile killing units) followed the German army, shooting over 1.3 million Jews in mass graves across Eastern Europe. The massacre at Babi Yar in Ukraine killed 33,771 Jews in just two days.

1942-1945: Death Camps

At the Wannsee Conference (January 1942), Nazi leadership coordinated the "Final Solution"—the systematic murder of all European Jews. Six extermination camps were established in occupied Poland: Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Chelmno, and Majdanek. Gas chambers and crematoria operated continuously to murder Jews on an industrial scale.

Major Locations

Auschwitz-Birkenau

The largest death camp, where approximately 1.1 million people were murdered, 90% of them Jews. Upon arrival, most were sent directly to gas chambers. Those selected for forced labor faced starvation, disease, and brutal conditions.

Warsaw Ghetto

The largest ghetto, where over 400,000 Jews were imprisoned in terrible conditions. In 1943, ghetto residents launched an armed uprising against deportations to Treblinka, fighting for nearly a month before the Nazis crushed the resistance.

Treblinka

A death camp where approximately 870,000-925,000 Jews were murdered between July 1942 and October 1943. Nearly all arrivals were sent directly to gas chambers disguised as showers.

Babi Yar

A ravine in Ukraine where 33,771 Jews were shot over two days in September 1941, one of the largest mass shootings of the Holocaust. Over 100,000 people total were killed at this site during the German occupation.

Liberation and Aftermath

As Allied forces advanced into Nazi-controlled territories in 1944-1945, they liberated concentration camps and discovered the full horror of the Holocaust. Soldiers found thousands of emaciated survivors, mass graves, and evidence of systematic murder.

The Nuremberg Trials (1945-1946) prosecuted major Nazi war criminals for crimes against humanity, establishing important precedents in international law.

Survivors faced the challenge of rebuilding shattered lives. Many had lost entire families and communities. Displaced Persons camps housed hundreds of thousands of survivors who had nowhere to return to.

The Holocaust led to the establishment of Israel in 1948 and fundamentally changed how the world understands genocide, human rights, and the need for vigilance against hatred.

Remembrance and Education

The imperative to remember the Holocaust—"Never Again"—carries both moral and practical weight. By preserving survivor testimony, maintaining historical sites, and educating new generations, we honor the victims and commit ourselves to preventing future genocides.
