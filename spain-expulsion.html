The Expulsion from Spain: 1492

On the same day Columbus set sail for the New World, hundreds of thousands of Jews were forced to abandon their homes, their heritage, and everything they had built over fifteen centuries. The Alhambra Decree ended one of history's most vibrant Jewish communities.

Overview

On March 31, 1492, Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile issued the Alhambra Decree (also called the Edict of Expulsion). It ordered all practicing Jews to leave Spain by July 31, 1492—a deadline later extended to August 2nd.

Spanish Jews—known as Sephardim—had lived in the Iberian Peninsula since Roman times. During the Islamic Golden Age (8th-12th centuries), Spanish Jewry flourished, producing scholars, poets, philosophers, and physicians who made lasting contributions to world civilization.

The expulsion followed the completion of the Reconquista—Christian reconquest of Spain from Muslim rule. The monarchs sought religious uniformity in their newly unified kingdom. Jews were given an impossible choice: convert to Christianity, leave Spain, or face execution.

An estimated 200,000-300,000 Jews refused conversion and left Spain. Many died during the journey from disease, starvation, or violence. Those who survived scattered across the Mediterranean, North Africa, and the Ottoman Empire, carrying with them the Ladino language and Sephardic traditions.

The Golden Age and Its End

The Golden Age (711-1250)

Under Muslim rule, Spanish Jews experienced a golden age of cultural and intellectual achievement. Jewish scholars, working alongside Muslim and Christian counterparts, made groundbreaking advances in philosophy, medicine, astronomy, and mathematics. Figures like Moses Maimonides (the Rambam), Judah Halevi, and Solomon ibn Gabirol produced works that remain influential today.

Decline and Persecution (1250-1492)

As Christian kingdoms expanded southward, conditions worsened for Jews. Anti-Jewish riots in 1391 killed thousands and forced many to convert. Increasing restrictions on Jewish life, combined with pressure from the Inquisition (established 1478), made Jewish existence increasingly precarious even before the expulsion decree.

The Expulsion Process

The Decree

The Alhambra Decree justified expulsion by claiming Jews influenced conversos to return to Judaism. It gave Jews four months to settle their affairs and leave. They were forbidden to take gold, silver, or currency with them—only goods that could be carried.

Economic Devastation

Jews were forced to sell property, businesses, and possessions at fraction of their value. Entire estates sold for the price of a donkey. Many buried treasures they couldn't carry, hoping to one day return. They never did.

The Journey

Refugees crowded onto ships, many unseaworthy. Some captains abandoned passengers on deserted shores. Others demanded additional payment mid-voyage. Pirates attacked refugee ships. Disease spread rapidly in overcrowded conditions. Thousands died before reaching their destinations.

Destinations

The Ottoman Empire welcomed Spanish Jews, recognizing their skills and knowledge. Sultan Bayezid II reportedly said: "You venture to call Ferdinand a wise ruler, he who has impoverished his own country and enriched mine!" Communities formed in Constantinople, Salonika, and throughout the Ottoman realm.

Historical Accounts

"A great cry went up from young and old. They raised their voices in weeping and mourning... Mothers fainted upon their sons, and fathers upon their daughters... Parents and children embraced each other weeping and took leave of one another."

— Contemporary chronicler describing the expulsion, 1492

Long-Term Impact

Spain's Loss

Spain lost skilled artisans, merchants, physicians, and scholars. The economic and cultural impact was profound. Some historians argue the expulsion contributed to Spain's eventual decline as a major European power.

Sephardic Diaspora

Expelled Jews established communities worldwide, creating a global Sephardic diaspora. They maintained Ladino (Judeo-Spanish) language and distinct Sephardic customs for centuries. Some communities kept keys to their Spanish homes for generations, symbols of an unrequited longing for return.

Conversos and Crypto-Judaism

Many who converted publicly continued practicing Judaism in secret. These crypto-Jews, or Marranos, lived in constant fear of the Inquisition. Some families maintained secret Jewish practices for generations, only revealing their heritage centuries later.

Modern Recognition

In 1992, 500 years after the expulsion, Spain formally revoked the Edict of Expulsion. In 2015, Spain and Portugal offered citizenship to descendants of expelled Jews—a symbolic gesture of reconciliation, though it cannot undo five centuries of exile.

Cultural Legacy

The expulsion from Spain produced a rich cultural legacy:

  • Ladino Language: Judeo-Spanish preserved medieval Spanish and Hebrew elements, still spoken by some Sephardic communities
  • Music and Poetry: Sephardic songs and romances passed down through generations preserve memories of Spain
  • Cuisine: Sephardic cooking blends Spanish, Mediterranean, and Middle Eastern flavors
  • Religious Traditions: Distinct Sephardic prayer rituals and customs developed in exile
  • Family Names: Many Sephardic surnames reflect Spanish origins (de Leon, Toledo, Cordova)
