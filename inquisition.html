The Spanish Inquisition: 1478-1834

For over three centuries, the Spanish Inquisition terrorized conversos—Jews who had converted to Christianity. Through torture, show trials, and public executions, it sought to root out those suspected of secretly practicing Judaism.

Overview

The Spanish Inquisition was established in 1478 by Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile. Its stated purpose was to maintain Catholic orthodoxy, but it primarily targeted conversos (also called "New Christians" or Marranos)—Jews who had converted to Christianity.

Many conversos had converted under duress to escape persecution, and some continued to practice Jewish rituals in secret. The Inquisition sought to identify and punish these individuals through a system of denunciations, torture, and public trials.

The Inquisition created an atmosphere of paranoia and terror. Families were torn apart by accusations, and neighbors denounced neighbors. Those convicted faced imprisonment, torture, confiscation of property, or execution.

The most infamous aspect was the auto-da-fé (act of faith)—elaborate public ceremonies where convicted heretics were sentenced and often burned at the stake. These spectacles served to intimidate the population and demonstrate the power of the Church and monarchy.

Methods and Procedures

Denunciation

Anyone could denounce someone to the Inquisition, often anonymously. The accused would be arrested and held in secret prisons, sometimes for years before trial. Property was confiscated immediately, leaving families destitute.

Torture

The Inquisition employed various torture methods to extract confessions: the rack, water torture, and the strappado. Torture sessions were carefully documented, and confessions obtained under torture were used as evidence.

Auto-da-fé

Public ceremonies where sentences were announced. Convicted heretics wore sanbenitos (penitential garments) and were paraded through the streets. Those sentenced to death were "relaxed" to secular authorities for execution, usually by burning at the stake.

Notable Cases and Figures

Tomás de Torquemada

The first and most notorious Grand Inquisitor (1483-1498). A Dominican friar, Torquemada was responsible for establishing the procedures and institutions of the Inquisition. Historians estimate that during his tenure, about 2,000 people were burned at the stake.

Signs of "Judaizing"

The Inquisition looked for signs that conversos were secretly practicing Judaism: lighting candles on Friday night, refusing to eat pork, wearing clean clothes on Saturday, preparing food according to Jewish law, or celebrating Jewish holidays. Even minor observances could lead to arrest.

Historical Testimony

"They came for us at night. My mother had lit candles—not for Sabbath, she said, but because we needed light. But they called it evidence. They took everything: our home, our belongings, our freedom. Torture made my father confess to things he never did."

— Testimony from an Inquisition trial record, 16th century

End and Legacy

The Spanish Inquisition was finally abolished in 1834, though it had declined in power during the 18th century. Its legacy includes:

  • The destruction of Spain's converso community
  • The establishment of a model for religious persecution used elsewhere
  • The spread of Inquisition practices to Spanish colonies in the Americas
  • Deep trauma in Sephardic Jewish communities worldwide
  • The term "inquisition" becoming synonymous with oppressive investigation

In recent years, Spain and Portugal have offered citizenship to descendants of Jews expelled or persecuted during this period, a small gesture of acknowledgment for centuries of persecution.
